### 1
Let  
$\alpha = a + bi$  
$\beta = c + di$  
where $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}$

$$
\alpha + \beta = (a + c) + (b + d)i
$$
$$
\beta + \alpha = (c + a) + (d + b)i
$$

Since addition in $\mathbb{R}$ is commutative:

$$
\alpha + \beta = \beta + \alpha \quad
$$

### 3
Let  
$\alpha = a + bi$  
$\beta = c + di$  
where $a, b, c, d \in \mathbb{R}$

**Compute $\alpha (\lambda \beta)$:**

$$
\alpha(\lambda \beta) = (a + bi)(\lambda c + \lambda di) = a \lambda c + a \lambda di + b i \lambda c + b i \lambda di
$$

$$
= \lambda(ac - bd) + \lambda(ad + bc)i
$$

Similarly,

**Compute $\lambda(\alpha \beta)$:**

$$
\lambda(\alpha \beta) = \lambda(ac - bd) + \lambda(ad + bc)i
$$

Thus:

$$
\alpha(\lambda \beta) = \lambda(\alpha \beta) \quad
$$

### 5
Let $\alpha = a + bi$

Define $\beta = -\alpha = -a - bi$  
Then:

$$
\alpha + \beta = (a + bi) + (-a - bi) = 0
$$

Thus, such $\beta$ exists.

**Uniqueness:**

Suppose $\gamma$ also satisfies $\alpha + \gamma = 0$.  
Then $\gamma = -\alpha = \beta$ by the **definition of additive inverse**, which is **unique**.

### 6
Let $\alpha = a + bi$, with $a, b \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\alpha \neq 0$.

By definition, $\frac{1}{\alpha}$ denotes the **unique** multiplicative inverse of $\alpha$, meaning:

$$
\alpha \left( \frac{1}{\alpha} \right) = 1
$$

**Explicit expression:**

$$
\frac{1}{a + bi} = \frac{a - bi}{a^2 + b^2} = \frac{a}{a^2 + b^2} - \frac{b}{a^2 + b^2}i
$$

Thus, $\beta$ exists and is **unique**.

### 7
We check that $\frac{-1 + \sqrt{3}i}{2}$ is a cube root of 1 by cubing it and verifying that the value is 1.

$$
\left( \frac{-1 + \sqrt{3}i}{2} \right) \left( \frac{-1 + \sqrt{3}i}{2} \right) = \frac{-1 - \sqrt{3}i}{2}
$$

$$
\left( \frac{-1 - \sqrt{3}i}{2} \right) \left( \frac{-1 + \sqrt{3}i}{2} \right) = 1
$$

### 8

Let:
$z = x + yi$
$where \( x, y \in \mathbb{R} \)$

Compute:

$$
z^2 = (x + yi)^2 = x^2 - y^2 + 2xyi
$$

We require:

$$
z^2 = 0 + 1i
$$

**Equating parts:**

(1) $x^2 - y^2 = 0$

(2) $2xy = 1$

From (1):

$$
x^2 = y^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad y = \pm x
$$

**Case 1: \( y = x \)**

Substitute into (2):

$$
2x^2 = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x^2 = \frac{1}{2}
$$
$$
x = \pm \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$

Thus:

$$
y = \pm \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}
$$

So one solution is:

$$
z = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}i
$$

**Case 2: \( y = -x \)**

Substitute into (2):

$$
2x(-x) = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad -2x^2 = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad x^2 = -\frac{1}{2}
$$

No real solution here since \( x \in \mathbb{R} \).

**Final Solutions:**

Thus, the only two roots are:

$$
z = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}i
$$
$$
z = -\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} - \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}i
$$

### 10
The scaling factor $\lambda$ applies uniformly to all components,  
so the ratios between the corresponding components must be the same —which in this case they are **not**.  
Thus, no such $\lambda$ exists.

In other words, looking at the first component:

$$
\lambda = \frac{12 - 5i}{2 - 3i}
$$

If we apply this $\lambda$ to the second component:

$$
\lambda \times (5 + 4i) = \frac{12 - 5i}{2 - 3i} \times (5 + 4i)
$$

This does not equal $7 + 22i$.

Therefore, we have a contradiction,  
and **no such $\lambda$ exists**.
